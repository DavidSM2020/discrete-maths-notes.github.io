Topic 06

Graphs & Networks

A graph is a set of vertices joined by edges — the simplest possible model of a network. This topic gives you the vocabulary to describe one, the matrix tools to store one, and the algorithms to decide whether it can be traversed or connected cheaply.

Why graphs matter

A graph throws away everything about a situation except what is connected to what. That is exactly the information you need for networks:

Computer networks

Routers are vertices, cables are edges. “Is there a route?” becomes “is there a path?”; “what is the cheapest network?” becomes a minimum spanning tree.

Social networks

People are vertices, friendships are edges. Degree measures popularity; connected components measure isolated groups.

Maps and logistics

Cities and roads, wells and pipelines, tasks and dependencies. Euler tours answer “can I traverse every road once?”; Hamiltonian tours answer “can I visit every city once?”.

The exam tests this topic in a small number of very predictable ways: degree sequences, girth, adjacency matrices, and classifying a graph as Eulerian or semi-Eulerian. The Exam Papers Guide collects the actual questions; this page supplies the theory behind them.

What is a graph?

Definition · Simple graph

A simple graph \(G\) consists of a non-empty finite set \(V(G)\) of vertices (or nodes) and a set \(E(G)\) of two-element subsets of \(V(G)\), called edges. We write \(G=(V,E)\).

Each edge \(\{u,v\}\) joins two distinct vertices \(u\) and \(v\). There is at most one edge between any pair, and no vertex is joined to itself.

A general graph relaxes both restrictions:

  • Parallel edges — two or more edges joining the same pair of vertices. Also called a multigraph.
  • Loop — an edge from a vertex to itself. A loop contributes \(2\) to the degree of that vertex.
  • Directed graph (digraph) — each edge has an arrow, so \((u,v)\) and \((v,u)\) are different. Used for relations in Topic 04.
  • Weighted graph — each edge carries a number (cost, distance, time). Minimum spanning trees use weights.

A general graph: a loop at \(u\), parallel edges between \(y\) and \(z\), and a vertex \(x\) of high degree.

Two trivial-but-useful special cases: the null graph \(N_n\) has \(n\) vertices and no edges; the empty graph is usually another name for it.

Walk vs trail vs path

They are nested: every path is a trail, and every trail is a walk, but not conversely. A walk may repeat edges and vertices; a trail may repeat vertices but not edges; a path repeats neither. Exam questions often say “path” when they mean “walk” — answer the definition, and state which one you are using.

Degree and the handshaking lemma

Definition · Degree

The degree of a vertex \(v\), written \(\deg(v)\), is the number of edges incident with \(v\). A loop contributes \(2\).

The degree sequence of a graph lists the degrees of all vertices in non-decreasing order, with repetitions.

For the graph in the figure above:

\[ \deg(u)=3,\quad \deg(v)=3,\quad \deg(w)=2,\quad \deg(x)=3,\quad \deg(y)=4,\quad \deg(z)=4 \]

so the degree sequence is

\[ (2,3,3,3,4,4) \quad\text{or, written non-decreasingly,}\quad 2,3,3,3,4,4. \]
Theorem · Handshaking lemma

In any graph,

\[ \sum_{v\in V(G)} \deg(v) = 2\,|E(G)|. \]

That is, the sum of all degrees is twice the number of edges. Consequently, the number of vertices of odd degree is even.

Why it is true. Every edge has two endpoints, so each edge contributes exactly 2 to the total degree count: once at each end. A loop contributes 2 at its single endpoint, which is why the convention “a loop counts twice” is forced on us.

This gives two instant checks on any degree sequence:

  • The sum of the degrees must be even.
  • Therefore it is impossible to have an odd number of odd-degree vertices.
Example · Edges from a degree sequence

A graph has degree sequence \((2,2,3,3,4)\). The sum is \(14\), so it has \(14/2=7\) edges.

The adjacency matrix in the 2022/23 exam had row sums \(2,2,3,3,4\); the same calculation gives \(7\) edges. You do not need to count entries in the matrix one by one.

Degree sequences and graphicality

A sequence of non-negative integers is called graphical if it is the degree sequence of some simple graph. The exam asks you to decide whether a given sequence is graphical and to justify the answer.

The quick checks

CheckWhat it rules out
Sum of degrees is evenSequences that would need half an edge.
Every degree \(0 \le d \le n-1\)A simple graph on \(n\) vertices has no vertex joined to itself, and at most \(n-1\) neighbours.
If \(d=n-1\) appears, then every other degree is at least 1A universal vertex is adjacent to everybody, so nobody can be isolated.
Havel–Hakimi reductionEverything else.
Method · Havel–Hakimi

To test whether \((d_1,\ldots,d_n)\) with \(d_1\ge d_2\ge\cdots\ge d_n\) is graphical:

  1. Remove the largest degree \(d_1\).
  2. Subtract \(1\) from the next \(d_1\) largest degrees.
  3. If any entry becomes negative, the sequence is not graphical.
  4. Reorder and repeat. The sequence is graphical exactly when you reach all zeros.
Example · the exam sequence \((3,3,5,5,5,5)\)

Write it in descending order: \((5,5,5,5,3,3)\). Remove the leading \(5\) and subtract 1 from the next five entries:

\[ (5,5,5,5,3,3) \;\longrightarrow\; (4,4,4,2,2). \]

Repeat: remove \(4\), subtract 1 from the next four:

\[ (4,4,4,2,2) \;\longrightarrow\; (3,3,1,1). \]

Repeat: remove \(3\), subtract 1 from the next three:

\[ (3,3,1,1) \;\longrightarrow\; (2,0,0). \]

Repeat: remove \(2\), subtract 1 from the next two entries. The second entry is \(0\), so it becomes \(-1\):

\[ (2,0,0) \;\longrightarrow\; (-1,-1). \]

A negative entry means no simple graph has this degree sequence. The answer to the exam question is therefore “No”, with the Havel–Hakimi reduction as the justification.

Faster reason. Four vertices have degree 5, so each of them is joined to all five other vertices. In particular, both degree-3 vertices are adjacent to all four degree-5 vertices, which already gives them degree at least 4 — contradicting degree 3. So no simple graph exists. Either argument earns the marks; Havel–Hakimi is the safest to write out.

Example · valid and invalid sequences

(a) \((1,1,2,2,3,4,4,5,5)\) has odd sum \(27\), so it is not graphical.

(b) \((2,2,2,3,5,6,6,6)\) has sum \(32\), even. Descending: \((6,6,6,5,3,2,2,2)\). Remove \(6\), subtract from the next six: \((5,5,4,2,1,1,2)\to(5,5,4,2,2,1,1)\). Remove \(5\): \((4,3,1,1,0,1)\to(4,3,1,1,1,0)\). Remove \(4\): \((2,0,0,0,0)\). Remove \(2\): \((-1,-1,0,0)\). Negative, so not graphical. (It also has a vertex of degree 6 in an 8-vertex graph, forcing all others to have degree at least 1 — but one has degree 0? No, the smallest is 2. Still, the reduction settles it.)

(c) \((1,2,3,4,5,6,7)\) has sum \(1+2+3+4+5+6+7=28\), which is even. But it has \(n=7\) vertices and a degree of 7, which is impossible in a simple graph (maximum \(n-1=6\)). So not graphical.

The pigeonhole proof

“A simple graph with at least two vertices has two vertices of the same degree.” There are only \(n\) possible degrees \(0,1,\ldots,n-1\), but degree \(0\) and degree \(n-1\) cannot both occur (an isolated vertex cannot coexist with a universal vertex). So at most \(n-1\) distinct degree values are available for \(n\) vertices — pigeonhole gives a repeat. This is a favourite short proof question.

Bipartite test

A graph is bipartite exactly when its vertices can be 2-coloured so that every edge joins different colours. Equivalently, it contains no odd cycle. So \(C_n\) is bipartite iff \(n\) is even, \(K_n\) is bipartite only for \(n\le2\), and every tree is bipartite.

Girth

Definition · Girth

The girth of a graph is the length of its shortest cycle. A graph with no cycle has no (finite) girth. A loop has girth 1 and a pair of parallel edges has girth 2, so only simple graphs have girth at least 3.

Example · the four graphs the exam always uses

\(K_{5,7}\)4Bipartite, so no odd cycle. Two vertices on one side plus two on the other make a 4-cycle.
\(C_8\)8The only cycle is the whole 8-cycle.
\(W_8\)3Two adjacent rim vertices plus the hub form a triangle.

Marks are usually \(2\) per graph: one for the number and one for identifying the cycle or the reason.

Adjacency and incidence matrices

Definition · Adjacency matrix

Label the vertices \(1,2,\ldots,n\). The adjacency matrix \(A\) is the \(n\times n\) matrix whose \((i,j)\)-entry is the number of edges joining vertex \(i\) to vertex \(j\).

  • A loop at \(i\) contributes \(2\) to \(a_{ii}\) (some texts use 1; the module uses 2).
  • Parallel edges produce an entry greater than 1.
  • For an undirected graph, \(A\) is symmetric: \(a_{ij}=a_{ji}\).
Theorem · Reading a graph from its adjacency matrix
  • The degree of vertex \(i\) is the sum of row \(i\) (equivalently, column \(i\)).
  • The number of edges is \(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}\), or half the sum of the degrees.
  • The graph is simple iff every entry is \(0\) or \(1\) and every diagonal entry is \(0\).
  • The entry \((A^k)_{ij}\) counts the number of walks of length \(k\) from \(i\) to \(j\).
Example · the 2022/23 exam matrix

The matrix

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix} 0&1&1&2&0\\ 1&0&0&0&1\\ 1&0&0&1&1\\ 2&0&1&0&0\\ 0&1&1&0&0 \end{pmatrix} \]

(i) Is \(G\) simple? No: the entry \(a_{14}=2\) means there are two edges between vertex 1 and vertex 4 (parallel edges). A simple graph would have only 0s and 1s.

(ii) Degree sequence? Row sums: \(0+1+1+2+0=4\); \(1+0+0+0+1=2\); \(1+0+0+1+1=3\); \(2+0+1+0+0=3\); \(0+1+1+0+0=2\). Sorted: \((2,2,3,3,4)\).

(iii) Number of edges? Sum of degrees \(=4+2+3+3+2=14\), so \(|E|=14/2=7\).

Notice that the diagonal is all zeros, so there are no loops. The only obstruction to simplicity is the 2.

Definition · Incidence matrix

Label the vertices \(1,\ldots,n\) and the edges \(e_1,\ldots,e_m\). The incidence matrix \(M\) is the \(n\times m\) matrix whose \((i,j)\)-entry is \(1\) if vertex \(i\) is incident with edge \(j\), and \(0\) otherwise. A loop contributes \(2\) in its column.

  • Every column sums to \(2\) — an edge has two endpoints.
  • Parallel edges give identical columns.
  • An all-zero row is an isolated vertex.

Walks, trails, paths, cycles, girth

The definitions are in the jargon table. The exam-relevant facts are:

  • A path has distinct vertices, so its length is at most \(n-1\).
  • A cycle in a simple graph has length at least 3 (a triangle), and at most \(n\).
  • A graph is connected iff every pair of vertices has a path between them.
  • A tree has exactly one path between every pair of vertices.
  • The girth is the length of the shortest cycle; a tree has none.
Example · reading a walk off a graph

In a graph with vertices \(v,w,x,y,z\), the sequence

\[ v\to w\to x\to y\to z\to z\to y\to w \]

is a walk of length 7: it repeats the edge \(y\!-\!z\) and the vertices \(y,z\). It is not a trail. By contrast \(v\to w\to x\to y\to z\) is a path of length 4, and \(v\to w\to x\to y\to v\) is a cycle of length 4.

Connectivity

A graph can fail to be connected in two ways: it can split into separate components, or it can be held together by a small number of edges or vertices. The exam occasionally asks for the connectivity numbers of standard graphs.

Definition · Cutset, bridge, connectivity
  • A disconnecting set is a set of edges whose removal disconnects the graph.
  • A cutset is a minimal disconnecting set — no proper subset disconnects the graph.
  • A bridge is a cutset with one edge.
  • The edge connectivity \(\lambda(G)\) is the size of the smallest cutset.
  • A separating set is a set of vertices whose removal disconnects the graph; a single such vertex is a cut-vertex.
  • The vertex connectivity \(\kappa(G)\) is the size of the smallest separating set.
Theorem · Whitney's inequality

For any connected graph \(G\),

\[ \kappa(G) \le \lambda(G) \le \delta(G), \]

where \(\delta(G)\) is the minimum degree. If \(G\) has a bridge then \(\lambda(G)=1\); if it has a cut-vertex then \(\kappa(G)=1\).

Example · connectivity of standard graphs
Graph\(\kappa(G)\)\(\lambda(G)\)Reason
\(C_6\)22Removing any one vertex or edge leaves a path, still connected; removing two opposite vertices disconnects it.
\(W_6\)33The hub plus two rim vertices disconnects the rim; rim vertices have degree 3, so \(\delta=3\).
\(K_{4,7}\)44Remove the 4 vertices on the smaller side to isolate the other side; \(\delta=4\).

Also useful: a simple graph on \(n\) vertices with more than \(\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}\) edges is automatically connected. If it had two components, putting all edges in the largest possible component would still not reach that many edges.

Eulerian graphs

Definition · Eulerian and semi-Eulerian

A connected graph \(G\) is Eulerian if there is a closed trail containing every edge exactly once. Such a trail is an Euler tour.

A non-Eulerian connected graph is semi-Eulerian if there is an open trail containing every edge exactly once.

Key point: an Euler tour traverses every edge once; vertices may repeat.

Theorem · Euler's criterion (1736)

A connected graph is Eulerian if and only if every vertex has even degree.

A connected graph is semi-Eulerian if and only if it has exactly two vertices of odd degree. Any Euler trail must start at one odd-degree vertex and finish at the other.

A connected graph with more than two odd-degree vertices has neither an Euler tour nor an Euler trail.

Why it is true. Every time a trail passes through a vertex it uses two edges — one in, one out. So all edges at an internal vertex pair up, forcing even degree. In a closed tour the start vertex also pairs up. In an open trail only the start and end have an unpaired edge, so exactly those two can be odd.

Fleury's algorithm

To actually draw an Euler tour: start anywhere, erase edges as you traverse them, and use a bridge only when there is no alternative. This guarantees you do not strand part of the graph. The exam rarely asks you to run it, but it explains why greedy wandering can fail.

Hamiltonian graphs

Definition · Hamiltonian

A connected graph is Hamiltonian if there is a closed trail that passes through every vertex exactly once (returning to the start). This is a Hamiltonian cycle.

A graph is semi-Hamiltonian if there is an open path through every vertex exactly once.

Euler vs Hamilton

Euler is about edges; Hamilton is about vertices. Euler has a clean iff criterion; Hamilton has no known simple necessary-and-sufficient condition, and deciding it is NP-complete. Do not apply Euler's degree test to a Hamiltonian question.

Theorem · Ore and Dirac

Ore (1960). If \(G\) is a simple graph with \(n\ge3\) vertices and \(\deg(v)+\deg(w)\ge n\) for every pair of non-adjacent vertices \(v,w\), then \(G\) is Hamiltonian.

Dirac (1952). If \(G\) is simple with \(n\ge3\) and \(\deg(v)\ge n/2\) for every vertex, then \(G\) is Hamiltonian.

These conditions are sufficient, not necessary: a graph can be Hamiltonian and fail them.

Example · which families are Hamiltonian?
  • \(K_n\) is Hamiltonian for every \(n\ge3\).
  • \(K_{r,s}\) is Hamiltonian iff \(r=s\ge2\). A Hamiltonian cycle must alternate sides, so the sides must be equal; \(K_{2,2}=C_4\) works.
  • \(W_n\) is Hamiltonian for every \(n\ge4\): go round the rim.
  • \(Q_k\) is Hamiltonian for every \(k\ge2\) (Gray code order).
  • A bipartite graph with an odd number of vertices cannot be Hamiltonian: a cycle alternates sides, so it has even length and uses equally many vertices from each side.

Trees and spanning trees

Definition · Tree, forest, spanning tree

A forest is a graph with no cycles. A connected forest is a tree.

A spanning tree of a connected graph \(G\) is a subgraph that is a tree and contains every vertex of \(G\). A minimum spanning tree (MST) is a spanning tree of minimum total edge weight.

Theorem · Characterisations of a tree

For a graph \(T\) with \(n\) vertices, the following are equivalent:

  1. \(T\) is a tree.
  2. \(T\) has no cycles and \(n-1\) edges.
  3. \(T\) is connected and has \(n-1\) edges.
  4. \(T\) is connected and every edge is a bridge.
  5. Every two vertices are joined by exactly one path.
  6. \(T\) has no cycles, but adding any new edge creates exactly one cycle.

Consequences you should be able to quote:

  • A tree on \(n\) vertices has exactly \(n-1\) edges and at least two leaves (for \(n\ge2\)).
  • Every connected graph has a spanning tree; you get one by repeatedly deleting an edge from a cycle.
  • A spanning tree of a connected graph on \(n\) vertices has \(n-1\) edges, whatever the graph.
  • An MST is not unique when several edges have the same weight, but its total weight is unique.

Kruskal's algorithm

Kruskal builds an MST greedily:

  1. Sort all edges by weight, smallest first.
  2. Consider each edge in order. Add it to the tree unless it creates a cycle.
  3. Stop when you have added \(n-1\) edges. The result is a minimum spanning tree.
Worked example · Kruskal on the lecture graph

Eight vertices, and the edges in increasing weight order:

Exam technique for Kruskal

Always show the sorted edge list and state “skip — cycle” for each rejected edge. Method marks are for the greedy decision, not just the final tree. When weights tie, either choice gives a valid MST; say so if asked “how many such trees?”.

Prim's algorithm is the other standard MST algorithm: start at any vertex and repeatedly add the cheapest edge that connects a new vertex to the growing tree. Both algorithms produce an MST; Kruskal is usually easier to carry out by hand when the edge list is given.

Exam question recipes

These are the four graph questions that have actually appeared. Each recipe is the exact sequence of steps that earns full marks.

1 · “Does a simple graph with this degree sequence exist?”

  1. Sort the sequence descending.
  2. Check the sum is even and every degree is at most \(n-1\).
  3. Run Havel–Hakimi until you reach zeros (yes) or a negative entry (no).
  4. Write one sentence: “A negative entry cannot be a degree, so no simple graph exists.”
  5. If asked to draw one, construct it from the successful reduction — add edges from the highest-degree vertex to the next highest.

2 · “Write down the girth of \(K_9\), \(K_{5,7}\), \(C_8\), \(W_8\).”

  1. \(K_n\): girth 3 for \(n\ge3\) (triangle).
  2. \(K_{r,s}\): girth 4 when both sides have at least 2 (no odd cycles, and a 4-cycle exists).
  3. \(C_n\): girth \(n\).
  4. \(W_n\): girth 3 (hub + adjacent rim vertices).
  5. Give the cycle or the reason, not just the number.

3 · “Here is an adjacency matrix…”

  1. Simple? Check every entry is 0 or 1, and the diagonal is all 0. Any 2 means parallel edges; any diagonal 1 means a loop.
  2. Degree sequence? Add each row, then sort the sums non-decreasingly.
  3. Number of edges? Add all degrees and divide by 2 (handshaking lemma).

4 · “Classify as Eulerian / semi-Eulerian / neither.”

  1. Check the graph is connected (ignore isolated vertices).
  2. Count the odd-degree vertices.
  3. 0 odd → Eulerian. Exactly 2 odd → semi-Eulerian. Otherwise → neither.
  4. For a named family, compute the degree pattern first: \(K_n\) all \(n-1\); \(C_n\) all 2; \(W_n\) hub \(n-1\), rim 3; \(K_{r,s}\) degrees \(s\) and \(r\).
Full worked answer · degree sequence (3,3,5,5,5,5)

Question. Draw a graph with degree sequence \((3,3,5,5,5,5)\). Does there exist a simple graph with this degree sequence? Justify your answer.

Answer. Sort descending: \((5,5,5,5,3,3)\). Apply Havel–Hakimi:

\[ (5,5,5,5,3,3)\to(4,4,4,2,2)\to(3,3,1,1)\to(2,0,0)\to(-1,-1). \]

The negative entry shows the sequence is not graphical, so no simple graph has degree sequence \((3,3,5,5,5,5)\). A general graph can have it: a vertex of degree 5 can be joined to all five others, and the remaining edges can be supplied with parallel edges if necessary.

  • State Ore's and Dirac's sufficient conditions for Hamiltonian graphs.
  • List the equivalent characterisations of a tree.
  • Run Kruskal's algorithm and state the MST weight.
  • Recognise basic NetworkX calls.

Common traps

  • Sorting the degree sequence in the wrong direction — the definition is non-decreasing, but Havel–Hakimi is easier descending.
  • Forgetting that a loop adds 2, not 1, to the degree.
  • Saying “no simple graph” without a reason. The negative entry in Havel–Hakimi is the reason.
  • Confusing girth with diameter. Girth is the shortest cycle, not the longest shortest path.
  • Saying \(W_n\) has an \(n\)-cycle. It has a hub plus an \((n-1)\)-cycle rim.
  • Using Euler's even-degree test for a Hamiltonian question.
  • Forgetting to divide the degree sum by 2 when counting edges.
  • Counting a walk as a path when it repeats a vertex.
  • Claiming an MST is unique when edge weights tie.
  • Drawing a graph with a degree larger than \(n-1\) — impossible in a simple graph.
Before you submit

Every graph answer should name the object and the test: “not simple because \(a_{14}=2\)”, “semi-Eulerian because exactly two vertices have odd degree”, “girth 4 because \(K_{5,7}\) is bipartite and contains a 4-cycle”. A bare number rarely gets full marks.
