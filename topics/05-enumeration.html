Topic 05

Enumeration

Enumeration is the art of counting without listing. Three basic principles — addition, multiplication and subtraction — combine into everything else: subsets, bit strings, lattice paths, the binomial theorem, Pascal's triangle, permutations and combinations.

Why counting matters

Counting is not just arithmetic. It answers questions that show up everywhere in computing:

Algorithm complexity

The running time of a loop is the number of times its body executes. Counting paths through code gives big-\(O\) estimates.

Probability

The probability of an event is (favourable outcomes) / (total outcomes). You cannot compute it without counting both.

Networks and security

The number of nodes, edges, routes, or possible passwords measures reliability and the size of an attack surface.

Addition principle

Definition · Addition principle

If event \(A\) can occur in \(m\) ways and event \(B\) can occur in \(n\) ways, and \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint (cannot both happen), then “\(A\) or \(B\)” can occur in

\[ m+n \]

ways. In set language, if \(A \cap B = \varnothing\), then \(|A \cup B| = |A| + |B|\).

Example · two-letter words

How many two-letter words start with either A or B?

There are 26 words starting with A (the second letter is free) and 26 starting with B. The two groups cannot overlap, so the total is

\[ 26 + 26 = 52. \]

The word starts with A exclusive or B — exclusive or maps to addition.

Non-example · the disjointness condition

A standard deck has 26 red cards and 12 face cards, but the number of cards that are red or a face card is not \(26+12=38\). Six cards are both red and face cards, so those are counted twice:

\[ 26 + 12 - 6 = 32. \]

This is the inclusion–exclusion correction. The addition principle only applies when the sets do not overlap.

Signal words

“Or”, “either … or”, “one of” usually signal addition — provided the cases are mutually exclusive. If they can overlap, subtract the overlap or switch to multiplication.

Multiplication principle

Definition · Multiplication principle

If event \(A\) can occur in \(m\) ways and, for each of those, event \(B\) can occur in \(n\) ways, then “\(A\) and \(B\)” can occur in

\[ m \cdot n \]

ways. Events must be independent: the number of options for \(B\) must not depend on which option was chosen for \(A\).

In set language, \(|A \times B| = |A||B|\).

Example · lunch combinations

A snack bar has 5 sandwiches and 3 drinks. A lunch is one sandwich and one drink:

\[ 5 \times 3 = 15 \text{ lunches}. \]

A tree diagram makes the structure explicit: 5 first-level branches, each splitting into 3.

Example · counting functions

How many functions \(f : \{1,2,3,4,5\} \to \{a,b,c,d\}\) are there?

Each of the 5 domain elements independently chooses one of 4 targets:

\[ 4 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 4^5 = 1024. \]

Example · a multiple-choice quiz

Four questions with 2 options each and three questions with 5 options each:

\[ 2^4 \times 5^3 = 16 \times 125 = 2000 \text{ answer sheets}. \]

Example · the power set

If \(|A|=n\), then \(|\mathcal{P}(A)| = 2^n\). For each of the \(n\) elements there are two independent choices: include it or do not include it. This is the multiplication principle with \(n\) events, each having 2 options.

Example · wardrobe

5 chinos, 3 check shirts, 13 t-shirts. A top is a check shirt or a t-shirt, so there are \(3+13=16\) tops. An outfit is a top and chinos:

\[ 5 \times 16 = 80 \text{ outfits}. \]

This combines both principles: add within a category, multiply across categories.

Example · two-digit numbers with distinct nonzero digits

A two-digit number \(ab\) has \(a \in \{1,\ldots,9\}\) and \(b \in \{1,\ldots,9\}\), with \(a \neq b\). There are 9 choices for \(a\), then 8 choices for \(b\):

\[ 9 \times 8 = 72. \]

The options for \(b\) are different for each \(a\), but there are always 8 — that is all the multiplication principle requires.

When multiplication fails

“How many ways can you pick two cards so the first is red and the second is a face card?” The answer is not \(26 \times 12\). If the first card is a red face card, only 11 face cards remain; otherwise 12 remain. The number of second choices depends on the first choice, so the simple multiplication principle does not apply directly. Split into cases instead.

Subtraction principle

Definition · Subtraction principle

For an event \(A\) inside a universal set \(U\),

\[ |A| = |U| - |A^{c}|. \]

Count the total, then subtract the outcomes you do not want. This is often easier when “bad” outcomes have a simpler description.

Example · repeated digits, again

Count two-digit strings with distinct nonzero digits by subtraction. There are 100 strings \(00,01,\ldots,99\). Remove:

  • 1 with double zero: \(00\);
  • 9 with a leading zero: \(01,\ldots,09\);
  • 9 with a trailing zero: \(10,20,\ldots,90\);
  • 9 with identical nonzero digits: \(11,22,\ldots,99\).

\[ 100 - 1 - 9 - 9 - 9 = 72. \]

Same answer as the multiplication method — a good check.

Example · passwords with a repeated symbol

A password is a 6-character string over 26 lowercase letters and 10 digits, so 36 symbols. Total passwords:

\[ |U| = 36^6 = 2{,}176{,}782{,}336. \]

Passwords with no repeated symbol:

\[ 36 \times 35 \times 34 \times 33 \times 32 \times 31 = 1{,}402{,}410{,}240. \]

Therefore passwords with at least one repeat:

\[ 2{,}176{,}782{,}336 - 1{,}402{,}410{,}240 = 774{,}372{,}096. \]

Example · fruit baskets

You have 6 identical oranges and 9 identical apples. A basket is determined by how many of each it contains: 7 choices for oranges \((0\)–\(6)\) and 10 for apples \((0\)–\(9)\), so \(7 \times 10 = 70\) baskets including the empty one. The problem forbids the empty basket, so

\[ 70 - 1 = 69. \]

Look for the complement

If a question says “at least one”, “not all”, or “contains a repeated …”, try counting the negation. Complements turn complicated unions into a single easy case.

Pigeonhole principle

Pigeonhole principle

If more than \(n\) pigeons fly into \(n\) pigeonholes, then at least one hole contains at least two pigeons.

Corollary · injection needs room

If \(f : A \to B\) is a function between finite sets and \(|A| > |B|\), then \(f\) cannot be injective.

Proof. There are \(|A|\) arrows leaving \(A\) but only \(|B|\) target elements. By the pigeonhole principle, some target receives at least two arrows, so \(f\) is not injective. \(\blacksquare\)

Example · remainders modulo 11

Pick any 12 positive integers. Then two of them differ by a multiple of 11.

Proof. Dividing by 11 leaves one of 11 possible remainders \(0,1,\ldots,10\). With 12 numbers and 11 remainders, two numbers share a remainder. Their difference is divisible by 11. \(\blacksquare\)

Reading the principle correctly

Pigeonhole gives a guarantee of a collision, not a way to find it. It is the standard tool for proving that two objects must share a property, and it is the theoretical reason hashing cannot be injective when the input space is larger than the output space.

Factorials

Definition · Factorial

For \(n \in \mathbb{N}\),

\[ n! = \begin{cases} 1, & n=0,\\ 1 \times 2 \times \cdots \times n, & n > 0. \end{cases} \]

Equivalently, \(n! = n \times (n-1)!\) for \(n \ge 1\), with \(0!=1\).

Values
\(n\)01234567
\(n!\)1126241207205040

Factorials grow extremely fast: \(10! = 3{,}628{,}800\), \(20! \approx 2.43 \times 10^{18}\), and \(52!\) is larger than \(8 \times 10^{67}\).

Useful factorial identity

If \(r \le n\), then

\[ \frac{n!}{r!} = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(r+1). \]

All the factors from \(r\) down to 1 cancel. This is why \(P(n,k)\) simplifies so neatly.

Factorial is not additive

\((m+n)! \neq m! + n!\) and \((mn)! \neq m!\,n!\). Factorial interacts with multiplication through \((n+1)! = (n+1)n!\), and with binomial coefficients through the definitions below. Do not invent rules.

Counting subsets

For a set \(A\) with \(n\) elements, every subset is determined by \(n\) independent yes/no decisions, so there are \(2^n\) subsets. But what if we fix the size?

Example · subsets of a 5-element set

Let \(A=\{1,2,3,4,5\}\). How many subsets of size 3?

First count ordered lists of 3 distinct elements: \(5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60\). But a set ignores order, and each 3-element set was counted once for each of its \(3! = 6\) orderings. Therefore

\[ \frac{5 \times 4 \times 3}{3 \times 2 \times 1} = \frac{60}{6} = 10. \]

Definition · Binomial coefficient

For integers \(n \ge 0\) and \(0 \le k \le n\), the number of \(k\)-element subsets of an \(n\)-element set is

\[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!}, \]

read “\(n\) choose \(k\)”.

The division by \(k!\)

The numerator counts ordered lists of length \(k\). Each unordered subset appears \(k!\) times in that count — once per ordering of its elements. Dividing by \(k!\) converts lists into sets.

Boundary values

\(\binom{n}{0}=1\) (only the empty set) and \(\binom{n}{n}=1\) (only the whole set). Also \(\binom{n}{k}=0\) for \(k<0\) or \(k>n\) if you extend the definition.

Symmetry

\(\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}\): choosing the \(k\) elements to include is the same as choosing the \(n-k\) to exclude.

Bit strings

Definition · Bit string

A bit string is a string of 0s and 1s. Its length is the number of bits; its weight is the number of 1s.

\(B_n\) denotes the set of all \(n\)-bit strings; \(B_n^k\) denotes those of weight \(k\).

Example

\(1001\) has length 4 and weight 2. \(0\) has length 1 and weight 0. \(1111\) has length 4 and weight 4.

\(B_5\) has \(2^5=32\) strings. \(B_5^3\) has \(\binom{5}{3}=10\) strings, because choosing a weight-3 string is exactly choosing which 3 of the 5 positions hold a 1.

Recurrence for bit strings

\[ |B_n^k| = |B_{n-1}^{k-1}| + |B_{n-1}^{k}|, \]

with terminal conditions \(|B_n^n|=1\) and \(|B_n^0|=1\).

Reason. An \(n\)-bit string of weight \(k\) either starts with 1 (then the remaining \(n-1\) bits need weight \(k-1\)) or starts with 0 (then the remaining bits need weight \(k\)).

This is the combinatorial proof of Pascal's identity, which appears again below.

Lattice paths

Definition · Lattice path

A lattice is a grid of points \((x,y)\) with integer coordinates. A lattice path is a shortest path between two lattice points moving only right (R) and up (U).

Counting lattice paths

The number of shortest paths from \(A=(a_x,a_y)\) to \(B=(b_x,b_y)\) is

\[ \binom{n}{k}, \qquad n=|b_x-a_x|+|b_y-a_y|, \quad k=|b_x-a_x|. \]

Here \(n\) is the total number of steps and \(k\) is the number of rightward steps. Equivalently, use the number of upward steps \(n-k\); the binomial coefficient is symmetric.

Example · \((0,0)\) to \((3,2)\)

Every shortest path has 3 right steps and 2 up steps, so length \(n=5\). A path is determined by which 3 of the 5 positions are R:

\[ \binom{5}{3} = 10. \]

For instance RRUUR, UURRR and RURRU are three of the ten.

Example · recursive decomposition

The last step into \((3,2)\) comes either from \((2,2)\) or from \((3,1)\). Therefore

\[ \binom{5}{3} = \binom{4}{2} + \binom{4}{3} = 6 + 4 = 10. \]

This is the same recurrence as Pascal's triangle, viewed geometrically.

Three views of one number

For \(n=5\) and \(k=3\), the number \(\binom{5}{3}=10\) counts simultaneously:

  • 3-element subsets of a 5-element set;
  • 5-bit strings of weight 3;
  • shortest lattice paths with 5 steps, 3 of them right.

Recognising which view a problem matches is most of the work.

The binomial theorem

Theorem · Binomial expansion

For \(n \ge 0\),

\[ (x+y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} y^{k}. \]

Equivalently, the coefficient of \(x^{n-k}y^k\) is \(\binom{n}{k}\).

Why? Expanding \((x+y)^n\) means choosing one of \(x\) or \(y\) from each of the \(n\) factors. A term \(x^{n-k}y^k\) arises exactly when \(y\) is chosen from \(k\) of the factors, and there are \(\binom{n}{k}\) ways to choose those factors.

Example · a coefficient with a constant

Find the coefficient of \(x^{12}\) in \((x+2)^{15}\).

Using the theorem with \(n=15\), the term containing \(x^{12}\) has \(k=3\):

\[ \binom{15}{3} x^{12} 2^{3} = 455 \cdot 8 \cdot x^{12} = 3640\,x^{12}. \]

So the coefficient is \(3640\).

Example · the row sum

Set \(x=y=1\):

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k} = (1+1)^n = 2^n, \]

which recovers the fact that an \(n\)-element set has \(2^n\) subsets. Setting \(x=1, y=-1\) gives the alternating sum \(\sum_k (-1)^k\binom{n}{k}=0\) for \(n \ge 1\).

Reading Pascal's triangle
  • The outer entries are always 1: \(\binom{n}{0}=\binom{n}{n}=1\).
  • Each row is symmetric: \(\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}\).
  • Row \(n\) sums to \(2^n\).
  • Row \(n\) has \(n+1\) entries, for \(k=0,\ldots,n\).

Permutations

Definition · Permutation

A permutation is a rearrangement of objects. Equivalently, it is a bijective function on a finite set.

The number of permutations of \(n\) distinct elements is

\[ n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots 2 \cdot 1. \]

Example · three letters

The permutations of \(a,b,c\) are

\[ abc,\ acb,\ bac,\ bca,\ cab,\ cba, \]

and there are \(3!=6\) of them: 3 choices for the first letter, 2 for the second, 1 for the third.

Definition · \(k\)-permutations

The number of ordered selections of \(k\) distinct objects from \(n\) distinct objects is

\[ P(n,k) = n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}. \]

Also written \({}_nP_k\). Note \(P(n,n)=n!\), and \(P(n,0)=1\).

Example · counting bijections

How many bijections \(f : \{1,\ldots,8\} \to \{1,\ldots,8\}\) are there?

Map 1 to any of 8 targets, 2 to any of the remaining 7, and so on:

\[ 8 \times 7 \times \cdots \times 1 = 8! = 40{,}320 = P(8,8). \]

Example · counting injections

How many injective functions \(f : \{1,2,3\} \to \{1,\ldots,8\}\) are there?

Injective means no target is reused, so

\[ 8 \times 7 \times 6 = \frac{8!}{5!} = P(8,3) = 336. \]

A bijection is impossible here because the target is larger than the source.

Example · dinner party

Choose 6 guests from 14 and seat them along a long table. Inviting is unordered, seating is ordered. The number of seating arrangements is

\[ P(14,6) = 14 \times 13 \times 12 \times 11 \times 10 \times 9 = 2{,}162{,}160. \]

If you only cared which 6 guests, the answer would be \(\binom{14}{6}=3003\).

Order matters

“Arrange”, “seat”, “rank”, “first/second/third”, “password”, “PIN” all signal that order matters — use permutations. “Choose”, “select”, “committee”, “subset”, “hand of cards” signal that order does not matter — use combinations.

Combinations

Definition · Combination

The number of unordered selections of \(k\) distinct objects from \(n\) distinct objects is

\[ C(n,k) = \binom{n}{k} = \frac{P(n,k)}{k!} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}. \]

Also written \({}_nC_k\) or \(\binom{n}{k}\).

The \(k!\) in the denominator corrects for over-counting: each unordered selection was counted \(k!\) times, once for each ordering of its elements.

Permutation

Ordered selection without repetition.

\[ P(n,k)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!} \]

“Pick a president, vice-president and treasurer.”

Combination

Unordered selection without repetition.

\[ C(n,k)=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} \]

“Pick a 3-person committee.”

Example · pizza menu

A parlour offers 10 toppings, no doubles.

  1. 3-topping pizzas: choose 3 of 10, order irrelevant: \[ \binom{10}{3}=120. \]
  2. All possible pizzas with 0 to 10 toppings: \[ \sum_{k=0}^{10}\binom{10}{k}=2^{10}=1024. \]
  3. Arrange 5 toppings in the left column of a two-column menu: choose and order 5 of 10: \[ P(10,5)=10\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7\cdot6=30{,}240. \]
Example · digits 2 through 8

How many 5-digit numbers can be formed from \(\{2,3,4,5,6,7,8\}\)?

  1. Repetition allowed, order matters: \(7^5=16{,}807\).
  2. No repetition, order matters: \(P(7,5)=7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3=2520\).
  3. No repetition, written in increasing order: the order is forced once the digits are chosen, so \(C(7,5)=\binom{7}{5}=21\).

(c) is a combination because choosing the set of digits uniquely determines the increasing arrangement; (a) and (b) are permutations/sequences because different orders give different numbers.

Which formula do I use?

Two questions decide almost every counting problem: does order matter? and is repetition allowed?

Repetition allowedNo repetition
Order matters\(n^k\)\(P(n,k)=\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}\)
Order irrelevantStars and bars (later topic)\(C(n,k)=\dbinom{n}{k}\)
  1. Read the question and decide whether two outcomes that differ only in order are considered different.
  2. Decide whether items may be reused. Passwords reuse; committees do not.
  3. Check for hidden structure: “at least one” suggests subtraction; “or” with overlap suggests inclusion–exclusion; a grid suggests lattice paths.
  4. Sanity-check the size: a count of subsets of a 5-element set cannot exceed 32; a probability numerator cannot exceed its denominator.

Mixed worked examples

Exercise · wardrobe

A wardrobe has 5 shirts, 3 pairs of pants and 17 bow ties. How many outfits (one of each) are possible?

Answer. \(5 \times 3 \times 17 = 255\).

Exercise · neckwear

You own 3 regular ties and 5 bow ties.

  1. How many choices of neckwear?
  2. How many if you wear one of each?
  3. With 5 shirts, 4 skirts, 3 pants and 7 dresses, how many outfits consisting of (shirt with skirt or pants) or a dress?

Answers. (1) \(3+5=8\). (2) \(3\times5=15\). (3) Shirt-and-bottom: \(5 \times (4+3)=35\). Dresses: 7. Total: \(35+7=42\).

Exercise · sets

If \(|A|=8\) and \(|B|=5\), what is \(|A \cup B| + |A \cap B|\)?

Answer. By inclusion–exclusion, \(|A \cup B| = |A|+|B|-|A \cap B|\), so \(|A \cup B|+|A \cap B| = 8+5 = 13\).

Exercise · five-letter words

Consider all 5-letter words over the letters \(a\) through \(h\) (8 letters).

  1. How many words in total?
  2. How many have no repeated letters?
  3. How many start with aha?
  4. How many start with aha or end with bah or both?

Answers. (1) \(8^5=32{,}768\). (2) \(P(8,5)=6720\). (3) The first three positions are fixed; the last two are free: \(8^2=64\). (4) Start with aha: 64. End with bah: 64. Both: the word must be ahabah, 1 word. Inclusion–exclusion gives \(64+64-1=127\).

Exercise · bit strings of length 9

How many 9-bit strings:

  1. start with 101?
  2. have weight 5 and start with 101?
  3. start with 101 or end with 11 (or both)?
  4. have weight 5, start with 101 and end with 11?

Answers. (1) \(2^6=64\). (2) Three 1s are used by 101; two more 1s are needed among the remaining 6 positions: \(\binom{6}{2}=15\). (3) Start 101: 64. End 11: \(2^7=128\). Both: first 3 fixed and last 2 fixed, middle 4 free: \(2^4=16\). Total \(64+128-16=176\). (4) First 3 and last 2 fixed use four 1s; one more 1 among the middle 4 positions: \(\binom{4}{1}=4\).

Exercise · lattice paths

How many shortest lattice paths start at \((3,3)\) and:

  1. end at \((10,10)\)?
  2. end at \((10,10)\) and pass through \((5,7)\)?
  3. end at \((10,10)\) and avoid \((5,7)\)?

Answers. (1) \(\Delta x=7\), \(\Delta y=7\), so \(n=14\), \(k=7\): \(\binom{14}{7}=3432\). (2) First leg \((3,3)\to(5,7)\): \(\Delta x=2\), \(\Delta y=4\), count \(\binom{6}{2}=15\). Second leg \((5,7)\to(10,10)\): \(\Delta x=5\), \(\Delta y=3\), count \(\binom{8}{5}=56\). Product: \(15 \times 56 = 840\). (3) Subtract: \(3432-840=2592\).

Exercise · binomial coefficient

What is the coefficient of \(x^{12}\) in \((x+2)^{15}\)?

Answer. \(\binom{15}{3}2^3=455 \times 8 = 3640\).

Exercise · subsets of \(\{1,\ldots,9\}\)
  1. How many subsets in total?
  2. How many contain exactly 5 elements?
  3. How many contain only even numbers?
  4. How many contain an even number of elements?

Answers. (1) \(2^9=512\). (2) \(\binom{9}{5}=126\). (3) There are 4 even numbers, so \(2^4=16\) subsets. (4) Exactly half of all subsets have even size: \(2^8=256\). (Pair each subset with its symmetric difference with \(\{1\}\).)

Counting in Python

Python's math module and itertools provide the standard counting tools. For learning, implement them yourself first; for exams, know what the built-ins compute.

factorial.pypython
# Iterative
def factorial_iterative(n):
    result = 1
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        result *= k
    return result

# Recursive
def factorial_recursive(n):
    if n == 0:
        return 1
    return n * factorial_recursive(n - 1)

for k in [0, 1, 5]:
    print(k, "! =", factorial_iterative(k))

# Built-in
import math
print(math.factorial(5))    # 120
choose.pypython
import math

def permutations(n, k):
    return math.factorial(n) // math.factorial(n - k)

def combinations(n, k):
    return math.factorial(n) // (math.factorial(k) * math.factorial(n - k))

print(permutations(14, 6))  # 2162160
print(combinations(14, 6))  # 3003

# Python 3.8+
print(math.perm(14, 6))     # 2162160
print(math.comb(14, 6))     # 3003

# [('a','b'), ('a','c'), ('b','c')]

# Count bit strings of length n with weight k
def count_weight(n, k):
    return len([s for s in range(2**n) if bin(s).count("1") == k])

print(count_weight(5, 3))   # 10
lattice.pypython
from functools import lru_cache

@lru_cache(maxsize=None)
def paths(x, y):
    """Shortest lattice paths from (0, 0) to (x, y)."""
    if x == 0 or y == 0:
        return 1
    return paths(x - 1, y) + paths(x, y - 1)

print(paths(3, 2))   # 10
print(paths(7, 7))   # 3432
Floating point danger

Do not compute binomial coefficients with floating-point factorials for large \(n\): math.factorial(100) / ... loses precision. Use integer division //, math.comb, or a multiplicative formula that keeps integers exact.

Exam checklist

Must be able to

  • State and apply the addition, multiplication and subtraction principles.
  • Recognise when events are disjoint or independent.
  • Apply the pigeonhole principle and state its injective-function corollary.
  • Compute factorials and simplify factorial ratios.
  • Count subsets of a given size with \(\binom{n}{k}\).
  • Count bit strings of a given length and weight.
  • Count shortest lattice paths, including paths through or avoiding a point.
  • Expand \((x+y)^n\) and extract a specific coefficient.
  • Use Pascal's identity and the symmetry \(\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}\).
  • Distinguish permutations from combinations and compute both.
  • Count injective and bijective functions between finite sets.
  • Combine principles in a multi-stage problem.

Common traps

  • Adding when the events overlap (red or face cards).
  • Multiplying when the number of second choices depends on the first choice.
  • Using \(n!\) when only \(k\) objects are being arranged — use \(P(n,k)\).
  • Using permutations when order is irrelevant — use \(C(n,k)\).
  • Forgetting to divide by \(k!\) when converting lists to sets.
  • Treating identical objects as distinct (or vice versa).
  • Counting the empty basket when the problem forbids it.
  • Using \(\binom{n}{k}\) with \(k>n\) or negative \(k\).
  • Forgetting that lattice paths must move only right and up.
  • Mixing up the total steps \(n\) and the right-step count \(k\).
  • Assuming \(\binom{n}{k}\) is the coefficient of \(x^k y^{n-k}\) rather than \(x^{n-k}y^k\) — read the exponent carefully.
  • Reporting a count larger than the total number of objects in the universe.
Before you submit

Write one sentence explaining why you multiplied, added or subtracted. Most marking schemes award method marks for the principle, not just the final number. Check small cases by hand: for \(n=3\) or \(n=4\), list the objects and compare with your formula.
