Logic

Logic is the science of reasoning: it gives statements an exact truth value and precise rules for combining them. This topic builds from the three basic connectives to truth tables, logical equivalence, conditionals, and finally quantifiers — the language every later proof is written in.

Propositions and truth values

Definition · Proposition

A proposition (or statement) is a sentence that is either true or false — never both, and never neither.

SentenceProposition?Why
“Java is a programming language.”Yes — trueIt makes a factual claim.
“Cork is the capital of Ireland.”Yes — falseFalse claims are still propositions.
“\(1+2=3\).”Yes — trueA mathematical statement.
“Today is Tuesday.”Yes — depends on the dayIts truth value is fixed once “today” is fixed.
“How are you?”NoA question has no truth value.
“Stop sleeping in class!”NoAn order has no truth value.
“Correct horse battery staple.”NoNot a sentence — no verb, no claim.
“This sentence is false.”NoPathological: assuming either value gives a contradiction.

The truth value of a proposition is written \(T\) / \(F\), or \(\mathsf{True}\) / \(\mathsf{False}\), or \(1\) / \(0\). Variables that stand for propositions are called propositional variables and are written with lower-case letters: \(p, q, r, s, p_1, p_2, \ldots\)

Atomic vs compound

An atomic proposition cannot be broken into smaller propositions. A compound proposition is built from atomic propositions using logical connectives (also called Boolean connectives or logical operators).

Example

“Today is wet and I am hungry” is compound: it joins the atomic propositions “Today is wet” and “I am hungry” with the connective and.

Exercise · classify

Classify each sentence as atomic, compound, or not a statement.

  1. The sum of the first 100 odd positive integers.
  2. Everybody needs somebody sometime.
  3. Waterford will win the All-Ireland or I'll eat my hat.
  4. Go to your room!
  5. Every natural number greater than 1 is either prime or composite.
  6. This sentence is false.

Answers. (1) Not a statement — it is a noun phrase, not a claim. (2) Atomic. (3) Compound — an or. (4) Not a statement — an order. (5) Atomic (the “either … or” is inside a quantified claim; it is one statement about all natural numbers). (6) Not a statement — the liar paradox.

And, or, not

These three connectives are enough to express every truth function. The truth value of a compound proposition is determined only by the truth values of its parts — this is the truth-functional nature of propositional logic.

Conjunction \(p \land q\)

“\(p\) and \(q\)”. True only when both are true. Python: and.

Disjunction \(p \lor q\)

“\(p\) or \(q\)”. True when at least one is true — this is inclusive or. Python: or.

Negation \(\neg p\)

“not \(p\)”. Flips the truth value. Python: not.

Double negation: \(\neg\neg p \equiv p\). In English: “It is not the case that it is not raining” means “it is raining”.

Inclusive or

Mathematical “or” is inclusive: \(p \lor q\) is true when both are true. The exclusive or (“one or the other, but not both”) is a different connective and is not one of the three basics.

Operator precedence

When brackets are omitted, bind in this order: \(\neg\) first, then \(\land\), then \(\lor\), then \(\to\), then \(\leftrightarrow\). In practice, always use brackets in your answers — it removes all ambiguity and makes your reasoning easy to mark.

Constructing truth tables

A propositional formula is a logical expression built from propositional variables and connectives. A truth table shows the value of the formula for every possible assignment of values to its variables.

Counting rows

A formula with \(n\) distinct atomic propositions has \(2^n\) rows in a complete truth table. With 2 variables: 4 rows; with 3 variables: 8 rows.

The four-step method

  1. Identify the constituent atomic propositions.
  2. List the compound sub-expressions in increasing order of complexity, ending with the whole formula.
  3. Enumerate every combination of truth values for the atomic propositions.
  4. Fill in each compound column row by row.
Reading tip

Build the table left to right. Never try to write the final column directly — compute the small pieces first, then combine them. This is exactly how you should lay out an exam answer.

Tautologies and contradictions

Definitions
  • Satisfiable: true for at least one assignment of its variables.
  • Tautology: true for every assignment. (Also written \(\models A\).)
  • Contradiction: false for every assignment.
  • Contingent: true for some assignments and false for others.

Tautology examples

\(p \lor \neg p\)   (“tomorrow I will be dead or I will be alive”)
\((p \land q) \lor (p \land \neg q) \lor \neg p\)
\(p \to (p \lor q)\)

Contradiction examples

\(p \land \neg p\)   (“I will win the lottery and not win the lottery”)
\((p \lor q) \land \neg p \land \neg q\)

Relationships

Every tautology is satisfiable. A contradiction is not satisfiable. A formula is a tautology iff its negation is a contradiction. “Satisfiable” asks whether the formula can be true somewhere; “tautology” asks whether it is true everywhere.

Testing a claim

  • To show a formula is a tautology, its final column must be all T.
  • To show it is a contradiction, its final column must be all F.
  • To show it is satisfiable, exhibit one row with T.
  • To show it is not a tautology, exhibit one row with F — a single counterexample is enough.

Logical equivalence

Definition · Logical equivalence

Two formulas are logically equivalent, written \(A \equiv B\), if they have the same truth value on every assignment of their variables. Equivalently, \(A \leftrightarrow B\) is a tautology.

There are two ways to prove an equivalence:

  1. Truth tables — construct both tables and compare the output columns. Easy, but long and error-prone.
  2. Algebraic laws — transform one formula into the other using known equivalences. Shorter, but you must know the laws.

De Morgan's laws

Theorem · De Morgan

\[ \neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q \] \[ \neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q \]

In words: “not (p or q)” means “neither p nor q”; “not (p and q)” means “at least one of p, q fails”.

Worked proof: \(\neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q\)

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \lor q\)\(\neg(p \lor q)\)\(\neg p\)\(\neg q\)\(\neg p \land \neg q\)

Exam strategy

If an equivalence question is worth few marks, use a truth table — it is mechanical and hard to get wrong if you are careful. If it asks you to “simplify” or gives many variables, use the laws.

Rules of inference

Equivalence laws let you rewrite a statement; rules of inference let you derive a new statement from premises. Write \(\Rightarrow\) for “therefore”. The official formula sheet attached to the exam lists these, so learn the names.

RulePatternExample
Modus ponens (detachment)\((p \to q) \land p \;\Rightarrow\; q\)If it rains, the ground is wet. It rains. Therefore the ground is wet.
Modus tollens (indirect reasoning)\((p \to q) \land \neg q \;\Rightarrow\; \neg p\)If it rains, the ground is wet. The ground is not wet. Therefore it did not rain.
Disjunctive addition\(p \;\Rightarrow\; (p \lor q)\)It is Monday. Therefore it is Monday or Friday.
Conjunctive simplification\((p \land q) \;\Rightarrow\; p\)  and  \((p \land q) \;\Rightarrow\; q\)It is Monday and raining. Therefore it is Monday.
Disjunctive simplification\((p \lor q) \land \neg p \;\Rightarrow\; q\)  and  \((p \lor q) \land \neg q \;\Rightarrow\; p\)It is Monday or Friday. It is not Monday. Therefore it is Friday.
Hypothetical syllogism (chain rule)\((p \to q) \land (q \to r) \;\Rightarrow\; (p \to r)\)If it rains the ground is wet; if the ground is wet the match is cancelled. Therefore rain cancels the match.
Resolution\((\neg p \lor r) \land (p \lor q) \;\Rightarrow\; (q \lor r)\)Used by automated theorem provers; combines two clauses, cancelling \(p\) against \(\neg p\).
Common fallacies

These look like rules but are invalid:

  • Affirming the consequent: \((p \to q) \land q \;\not\Rightarrow\; p\). Wet ground does not prove rain.
  • Denying the antecedent: \((p \to q) \land \neg p \;\not\Rightarrow\; \neg q\). No rain does not prove the ground is dry.

Conditionals: if … then

Definition · Conditional

The conditional (implication, if-then) is written \(p \to q\), where \(p\) is the hypothesis (antecedent) and \(q\) is the conclusion (consequent).

\[ p \to q \text{ is } F \text{ only when } p \text{ is } T \text{ and } q \text{ is } F; \text{ otherwise } T. \]

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \to q\)
FFT
FTT
TFF
TTT

The motivating example from the lectures: “If Sandra gets at least 80% in the exam, then Sandra will pass the module.” This claim is only broken when Sandra gets at least 80% and still fails. If she gets less than 80%, the promise says nothing about her result — she might still pass, but the implication is not falsified.

Vacuous truth

An implication is true whenever its hypothesis is false. This surprises people. “If fish have hair, then chickens have lips” is a true implication, because the hypothesis is false. The two clauses need not be causally or thematically related — the conditional is a truth function, not a claim about cause and effect.

Worked examples

“\(0=1 \to 1=1\)”TrueHypothesis false — vacuously true.

“If 8 is prime, then the 7624th digit of \(\pi\) is 8.”True8 is not prime — hypothesis false.
“If the 7624th digit of \(\pi\) is 8, then \(2+2=4\).”TrueConclusion true — the implication holds regardless of the digit.

“Only if” is not “if”

“\(p\) only if \(q\)” means \(p \to q\). “\(p\) if \(q\)” means \(q \to p\). They are converses, not the same statement. Read “only if” as “\(p\) can happen only when \(q\) happens”.

Biconditionals: iff

Definition · Biconditional

The biconditional (if and only if, iff) is written \(p \leftrightarrow q\) and is true exactly when \(p\) and \(q\) have the same truth value.

\[ p \leftrightarrow q \equiv (p \to q) \land (q \to p) \]

\(p\)\(q\)\(p \leftrightarrow q\)
FFT
FTF
TFF
TTT

Two formulas \(A\) and \(B\) are logically equivalent exactly when \(A \leftrightarrow B\) is a tautology. To prove an “iff” statement in mathematics you must prove both directions: \(A \to B\) and \(B \to A\).

Wording

“\(p\) if and only if \(q\)” is often abbreviated “\(p\) iff \(q\)”. Other phrasings: “\(p\) is necessary and sufficient for \(q\)”, “\(p\) exactly when \(q\)”.

Converse, contrapositive, inverse

Given the implication \(p \to q\), there are three related implications. Only one of them is equivalent to the original.

NameFormEquivalent to \(p \to q\)?
Original\(p \to q\)—
Converse\(q \to p\)No
Contrapositive\(\neg q \to \neg p\)Yes — always
Inverse\(\neg p \to \neg q\)No
Key facts
  • An implication and its contrapositive are logically equivalent.
  • The converse is not equivalent to the original. The converse of the contrapositive is the inverse, and vice versa.
  • The original and the converse are both true exactly when the biconditional \(p \leftrightarrow q\) holds.

Worked example

Let \(p\) = “\(x\) is a healthy horse” and \(q\) = “\(x\) has four legs”. The original is

\[ p \to q: \quad \text{“If } x \text{ is a healthy horse, then } x \text{ has four legs.”} \]

  • Converse \(q \to p\): “If \(x\) has four legs, then \(x\) is a healthy horse.” False for a four-legged aardvark.
  • Contrapositive \(\neg q \to \neg p\): “If \(x\) does not have four legs, then \(x\) is not a healthy horse.” Same truth value as the original.
  • Inverse \(\neg p \to \neg q\): “If \(x\) is not a healthy horse, then \(x\) does not have four legs.” Not equivalent to the original.

The Bob example

Suppose I tell Bob: “If you get 93% in the exam, then you will get an A in the module.” Assume this is true.

What you learnConclusion
Bob gets 93%.Bob gets an A. (Modus ponens: from \(p\) and \(p \to q\), infer \(q\).)
Bob gets an A.Nothing about his mark. He might have got an A another way.
Bob does not get 93%.Nothing about his grade. The promise is silent.
Bob does not get an A.Bob did not get 93%. (Modus tollens: from \(\neg q\) and \(p \to q\), infer \(\neg p\).)
Mnemonic

From \(p \to q\): affirm the hypothesis to get the conclusion; deny the conclusion to get the denial of the hypothesis. Affirming the conclusion or denying the hypothesis proves nothing — those are the classic fallacies.

Necessary and sufficient

Definition · Necessary and sufficient
  • “\(p\) is necessary for \(q\)” means \(q \to p\). Without \(p\), \(q\) cannot happen.
  • “\(p\) is sufficient for \(q\)” means \(p \to q\). \(p\) is enough to guarantee \(q\).
  • “\(p\) is necessary and sufficient for \(q\)” means \(p \leftrightarrow q\).
StatementTranslationEveryday reading
Being 18 is necessary for voting\(\text{vote} \to 18^+\)You cannot vote unless you are 18.
Scoring 50% is sufficient to pass\(50\% \to \text{pass}\)50% guarantees a pass.
Scoring 40% is necessary and sufficient\(40\% \leftrightarrow \text{pass}\)You pass exactly when you score 40%.
Common error

“Necessary” and “sufficient” are not interchangeable, and the direction is easy to reverse. Write the arrow immediately: necessary for \(q\) means the arrow points into \(p\) (\(q \to p\)); sufficient for \(q\) means the arrow points into \(q\) (\(p \to q\)).

Quantifiers

Quantifiers turn a predicate — a statement depending on a variable, such as “\(x > 0\)” — into a proposition. There are two in mathematics.

Definition · Quantifiers
  • Universal quantifier \(\forall\), read “for all” or “every”: \(\forall x\,[P(x)]\) asserts \(P(x)\) is true for every \(x\) in the domain.
  • Existential quantifier \(\exists\), read “there exists” or “there is”: \(\exists x\,[P(x)]\) asserts \(P(x)\) is true for at least one \(x\) in the domain.

To prove \(\forall x\,P(x)\)

You must show \(P\) holds for every element. One counterexample is enough to disprove it.

To prove \(\exists x\,P(x)\)

You only need to find one witness. To disprove it, you must show \(P\) fails for every element.

The standard notation

Always write predicates in the form

\[ \underbrace{\forall x}_{\text{quantifier + variable}} \underbrace{[\, x > 0 \,]}_{\text{statement}} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}\,[\, x > 0 \,] \]

where the optional \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) is the domain — the set the variable is drawn from. The square brackets are not mathematically required, but they make the scope of the quantifier unambiguous.

Translating English to predicates

Example 1

“Every number is positive.”

Reword: “For any number \(x\), \(x\) is positive.” Symbolically:

\[ \forall x\,[x > 0] \]

This is false over the integers or reals, because \(x = -1\) is a counterexample.

Example 2

“Every positive number is positive.”

Reword: “For any number, if the number is positive, then it is positive.” The hidden conditional is essential:

\[ \forall x\,[(x > 0) \to (x > 0)] \]

This is a tautology (true for every \(x\)), because the hypothesis and conclusion are identical. The lesson: “every \(A\) is \(B\)” becomes \(\forall x\,[A(x) \to B(x)]\), not \(\forall x\,[A(x) \land B(x)]\).

Example 3

“Some numbers are positive.”

Reword: “There exists at least one number such that it is positive.”

\[ \exists x\,[x > 0] \]

True over the integers: \(x = 1\) is a witness.

Example 4

“The average of two positive numbers is positive.”

Reword: “For all two numbers, if they are both positive, then their average is positive.”

\[ \forall x\,\forall y\,\big[((x > 0) \land (y > 0)) \to \tfrac{x+y}{2} > 0\big] \]

True over the reals.

The two translation patterns

“All \(A\)s are \(B\)” \(\;\longrightarrow\;\) \(\forall x\,[A(x) \to B(x)]\).
“Some \(A\) is \(B\)” \(\;\longrightarrow\;\) \(\exists x\,[A(x) \land B(x)]\).
Mixing these up (using \(\land\) with \(\forall\), or \(\to\) with \(\exists\)) is the single most common translation error.

Domain of discourse

The domain of discourse is the collection the variable ranges over. A quantified statement has no truth value until the domain is fixed.

Example · quantifier order matters

Consider \(\forall x\,\exists y\,[y < x]\).

  • Over the natural numbers \(\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,\ldots\}\) this is false: take \(x = 0\); there is no natural number \(y < 0\).
  • Over the integers \(\mathbb{Z}\) it is true: given \(x\), choose \(y = x - 1\).

Its negation is \(\exists x\,\forall y\,[y \ge x]\) — “there is a smallest number”. True over \(\mathbb{N}\), false over \(\mathbb{Z}\).

Theorem · \(\forall\) and \(\exists\) do not commute

In general,

\[ \forall x\,\exists y\,P(x,y) \;\not\equiv\; \exists y\,\forall x\,P(x,y). \]

The second statement is stronger: it demands a single \(y\) that works for every \(x\). The first allows \(y\) to depend on \(x\).

Example

Over the real numbers:

  • \(\forall x\,\exists y\,[y > x]\) is true: given \(x\), take \(y = x + 1\).
  • \(\exists x\,\forall y\,[y > x]\) is false: no real \(x\) is less than every real \(y\) (take \(y = x - 1\)).
Example · the domain changes everything
  • \(\forall x\,\forall y\,[x^2 + y^2 \ge 0]\) is true over \(\mathbb{R}\), but false over \(\mathbb{C}\) (take \(x = i\), \(y = 0\): \(i^2 + 0 = -1 < 0\)).
  • \(\exists x\,\exists y\,[x + 2y = 7]\) is true over \(\mathbb{R}\) — for example \(x = 3\), \(y = 2\).
Course convention

In this module, an unqualified “number” usually means a natural number \(\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,\ldots\}\), unless the question says integers, reals, or similar. State your domain explicitly in exam answers.

Negating quantified statements

Theorem · quantifier negation

\[ \neg\forall x\,P(x) \;\equiv\; \exists x\,\neg P(x) \] \[ \neg\exists x\,P(x) \;\equiv\; \forall x\,\neg P(x) \]

Negating a quantifier swaps it: \(\forall \leftrightarrow \exists\).

The intuition: “not everything has property \(P\)” means “something fails to have \(P\)”; “there is nothing with property \(P\)” means “everything fails to have \(P\)”.

Negating “all A are B”

The negation of “all \(A\)s are \(B\)” is “some \(A\) is not \(B\)” — a single counterexample. It is not “no \(A\)s are \(B\)”. Formally, \(\neg\forall x\,[A(x) \to B(x)] \equiv \exists x\,[A(x) \land \neg B(x)]\).

Worked puzzle: the three trolls

Three identical trolls guard a bridge. Each is either a knight (always tells the truth) or a knave (always lies). Number them left to right. Their statements are:

  • Troll 1: “If I am a knave, then there are exactly two knights here.”
  • Troll 2: “Troll 1 is lying.”
  • Troll 3: “Either we are all knaves or at least one of us is a knight.”

Solution

  1. Troll 3 must be a knight. The statement “either we are all knaves or at least one of us is a knight” is a tautology: if it is not the case that all are knaves, then at least one is a knight. A knave would have to utter a falsehood, but the statement cannot be false. So Troll 3 is a knight.
  2. Suppose Troll 1 is a knave. Then Troll 1's implication must be false. Its hypothesis “I am a knave” is true, so its conclusion must be false: there are not exactly two knights. But Troll 3 is already a knight, so the only way to avoid exactly two knights is for Troll 2 to be a knave. Then Troll 2's claim “Troll 1 is lying” would be true (Troll 1 is a knave), which contradicts Troll 2 being a knave. So Troll 1 cannot be a knave.
  3. Therefore Troll 1 is a knight. Its hypothesis “I am a knave” is false, so the implication is vacuously true — consistent with Troll 1 telling the truth.
  4. Troll 2 is a knave. Troll 2 says Troll 1 is lying, which is false because Troll 1 is a knight. A false statement is exactly what a knave says.
Answer

Troll 1: knight. Troll 2: knave. Troll 3: knight.

Check: Troll 1's conditional has a false hypothesis, so it is true; Troll 2's claim is false; Troll 3's disjunction is true. Every troll behaves according to its type.

Puzzle strategy

For knight/knave puzzles: (1) look for statements that are tautologies or contradictions — they force a type; (2) case-split on one character and propagate; (3) remember a knave's statement is false, so the negation of what they said is true.

Logic in Python

Python's Boolean operators are the connectives under different names, and they short-circuit: and stops at the first false operand, or stops at the first true one.

logic.pypython
p = True
q = False

print(p and q)      # False
print(p or q)       # True
print(not p)        # False
print(not (p and q))  # True  — De Morgan: (not p) or (not q)

# Short-circuit: the right-hand side may never run
def boom():
    raise RuntimeError("never called")

print(False and boom())   # False, boom() is skipped
print(True or boom())     # True,  boom() is skipped

A conditional is often written as an if statement, but it can also be written as a Boolean expression using the equivalence \(p \to q \equiv \neg p \lor q\):

implies.pypython
def implies(p, q):
    return (not p) or q

print(implies(False, False))   # True  — vacuous truth
print(implies(True, False))    # False — the only failing case
Truthiness warning

Python's and / or return an operand, not necessarily a Boolean. For example 0 or "yes" evaluates to "yes", and "a" and "b" evaluates to "b". Wrap the expression in bool(...) if you need a strict True/False.

Exam checklist

Must be able to

  • Decide whether a sentence is a proposition.
  • Write a full truth table for a formula with up to 3 variables.
  • Classify a formula as tautology, contradiction, or contingent.
  • Prove or disprove a logical equivalence.
  • Apply De Morgan's laws in both directions.
  • Name and apply the standard rules of inference.
  • Spot the fallacies of affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent.
  • State the truth table and equivalent form of \(p \to q\).
  • Write the converse, contrapositive, and inverse.
  • Translate “necessary” and “sufficient” into arrows.
  • Translate English sentences into predicates and back.
  • Negate a quantified statement correctly.

Common traps

  • Thinking a false hypothesis makes an implication false — it makes it true.
  • Confusing the converse with the contrapositive.
  • Writing “all \(A\) are \(B\)” as \(\forall x\,[A(x) \land B(x)]\).
  • Forgetting to swap \(\forall\) and \(\exists\) when negating.
  • Negating “all \(A\) are \(B\)” as “no \(A\) are \(B\)”.
  • Assuming \(\forall x\exists y\) and \(\exists y\forall x\) mean the same thing.
  • Using inclusive “or” when the English intends exclusive “or”.
  • Leaving out brackets and relying on precedence.
Before you submit

Check every truth table has \(2^n\) rows, every row has a value in every column, and the final column is what the question actually asked for. For quantified answers, state the domain.
